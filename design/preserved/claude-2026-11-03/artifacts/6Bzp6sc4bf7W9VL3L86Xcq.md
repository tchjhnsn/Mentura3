## 1. The function

Investor relations is part of a larger function: **capital**, meaning how the venture gets the money it needs and what it gives up in return. In a solo venture, you own it, like every other function. It sits next to finance (the money you have and spend) and depends on the strategic foundation (vision, phase, products), because investors are buying a share of where the venture is going.

The function has five jobs, and they come in this order:

1. **Know what you need.** What outcomes money would buy, and how much that costs. Until this is clear, you're reacting to offers instead of choosing them.
2. **Know who you're talking to.** Every investor has a type (angel, accelerator, venture studio, family office, fund) and each type wants different things.
3. **Tell the story.** Problem, why now, why you, what you've learned, and what you're testing next. In your own words.
4. **Do the deal.** Instruments, terms and signatures. This is where a lawyer comes in.
5. **Keep the relationship.** After a check clears: regular updates, honest bad news, and the cap table kept clean.

At Discovery, jobs 1 to 3 are most of the work. Job 4 happens only when someone actually offers money. Job 5 starts the day you meet an investor, before any money, because the best raises come from people who watched you make progress over months.

This is the same rule you gave for the funding decision on 2026-10-28: decide what the current phase needs and only speculate about later ones. Investor relations follows it well. You don't need a Series A plan; you need to know what gets you through Validation.

## 2. What investors will ask, and honest Discovery answers

Pre-seed investors mostly ask about the founder and the problem, because there's no revenue to look at yet. The answers below are scaffolds, not scripts. Your words go in the blanks, and anything marked as a decision waits for you.

| They ask | What they're really checking | A Discovery-honest shape for your answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What problem do you solve, and for whom? | Do you understand a real pain? | Your problem statement (2026-10-28): builders can now ship apps, but ventures stall with no users, no strategy and no clear next step. Name the founders you've talked to. |
| Why you? | Founder-market fit | Why you, in your own words: you've lived this across several ventures, and you've built the method (SAD, MAD) for it. |
| What have you learned so far? | Do you learn fast? | Two or three things founders told you that surprised you. If you don't have them yet, that's what Discovery is for. |
| How will you make money? | Do you have a credible path? | "I'm testing it. My leading hypotheses are X and Y, and here's how I'll test them." Investors at this stage expect hypotheses, not a settled model. (Docket decision.) |
| What's the vision? How big can this get? | Is it venture-scale? | Leave this fail open, as you asked. An honest answer: "I'm still forming it. Here's what I see today." Don't borrow someone else's bigger version. |
| Who else does this? | Do you know the market? | Name the nearest alternatives (startup playbooks, accelerators, AI coding agents) and the gap you see. |
| How much are you raising, and for what? | Is the ask tied to milestones? | Outcomes first (section 3). A number only once you've decided it. |
| Is it open source? | Defensibility and business model | "I haven't decided what's open yet. It's one of my next decisions." (Docket decision.) |
| Who's on the team? | Can you execute alone? | Solo, with agents as your team. Name any advisors only if they've agreed. |
| What do you need besides money? | Can they add value? | Intros to founders for Discovery, and advice on going to market. |

**Talking about money before monetization is settled.** Three moves keep you honest and credible:

- Separate what you know (problem, users, what they said) from what you're testing (pricing, who pays).
- Name the experiment, not the answer: "Five design partners will tell me whether founders pay for the framework, the platform, or the help."
- Never invent numbers. A made-up market size or revenue projection hurts you more than "I don't know yet."

On your fear that others' vision for Naive will outgrow yours: investors pitching you a bigger version is a common pattern. Their excitement is a signal worth writing down, but it isn't a mandate. You're right that the founder has to own the vision. That's why it stays in your words, and why it's fine to say it isn't finished.

## 3. Sizing a raise from outcomes, not activities

The amount comes last. Gordon Doughtery's point holds: "I'll hire a marketer" is an activity; "20 founders complete a venture package and 5 pay for it" is an outcome. Investors fund outcomes because outcomes are what make the next round possible.

Work it out in five steps:

1. **Name the milestone that ends the phase.** For you, that's exiting Discovery and then proving Validation. Write what evidence would prove each one (the `exit-discovery` decision is already in your docket, deferred).
2. **List what has to be true to reach it.** For example: design partners using Naive weekly, a pricing test run, the hackathon demo turned into a usable release.
3. **Cost each one.** Your living costs, tools and model spend, any contractor, legal and accounting. Use real quotes where you can.
4. **Add time and a buffer.** Most guidance says to fund 12 to 18 months of work plus a margin, since things take longer than planned (approximate, general guidance).
5. **Check the dilution.** Pre-seed rounds commonly sell roughly 10 to 20% of the company (approximate). If the amount you need would sell much more, either the milestone is too big for one round or the price is too low.

**Raising less is often safer.** A small first check (an accelerator or one or two angels) buys time to learn without locking in a valuation for a venture whose vision you haven't settled.

## 4. Instruments and contracts

Most pre-seed money in the US comes through a **SAFE**, and most of the risk lives in the terms and letters attached to it.

| Instrument or term | What it is | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Post-money SAFE (YC standard) | Money now for shares later, when you raise a priced round. The valuation cap fixes the investor's share: investment divided by post-money cap. | The cap is the price. A $250k SAFE at a $2.5M post-money cap is 10% of the company. Add up all your SAFEs before signing another. |
| Valuation cap | The maximum valuation the SAFE converts at. | Too low gives away too much; too high can make your next round awkward. |
| Discount | Investor converts at a discount (often around 20%) to the next round's price. | With both a cap and a discount, the investor gets whichever is better for them. |
| Uncapped MFN SAFE | No cap; it adopts the best terms you give any later SAFE investor. | Common in accelerator deals, including Antler's US package. Fine in itself, but every better deal you give later flows back to it. |
| MFN (most favored nation) clause | An investor can take any better terms you give someone later. | Narrow it: a time window, and economic terms only. |
| Pro rata side letter | The right to invest more in your next round to keep their percentage. | Reasonable for meaningful checks. Don't give it to every small check, and keep it personal so it doesn't transfer with the SAFE. |
| Information rights | The right to regular financial updates. | Fine; you should send updates anyway. |
| Convertible note | Like a SAFE, but it's debt, with interest and a maturity date. | Maturity can force repayment or conversion at a bad time. SAFEs are simpler at your stage. |
| Priced round (equity) | Shares sold at a set valuation, often with a board seat. | Usually seed or later. Board seats and control terms matter much more here. |
| Venture studio or services-for-equity | A studio gives work, people or network for a stake. | Studios that co-found can take 30% or more. Ask what is funded in cash versus provided as services, and how vesting works. |
| Advisor agreement | Small equity (often under 1%, vesting) for advice. | Vest it over time, with a cliff. Never give equity for intros alone. |
| Design partner agreement | A first-user relationship: product access for feedback. | Covered in section 8; it shouldn't include equity. |

**Contracts you'll need before taking any money**, all for a lawyer to set up: the company itself (most US venture investors expect a Delaware C-corp), your founder shares with vesting, an IP assignment from you to the company (so Naive and Mentura belong to the venture, not to you personally), and a cap table. An investor will check each of these before wiring money.

## 5. Red flags, and rules that prevent a bad deal

A bad early deal is usually one of four things: too much of the company sold too cheaply, control given away, rights that scare off later investors, or a partner whose interests compete with yours.

**Red flags**

- Pressure to sign today, or an "exploding" offer.
- A custom document instead of a standard SAFE, or changes to the YC form you can't explain.
- Board seats, vetoes or control rights at pre-seed.
- Equity for promises ("I'll make intros") rather than cash or clearly defined, vesting work.
- Rights over your IP, or a claim on future products.
- Big pro rata, super pro rata, or transferable side letters for a small check.
- An investor who builds something close to yours and asks for your roadmap or code.
- A stake large enough (with your vision still forming) that someone else would end up steering the venture.

**Rules for yourself (proposed, yours to adopt)**

1. Sign nothing in the room. Sleep on every offer for at least 48 hours.
2. Use standard documents (the YC post-money SAFE) and have a startup lawyer review every one.
3. Keep a running total of what you've sold on a single cap table page, and check it before each new check.
4. Ask every investor for two founders they've backed, and call them.
5. Share demos freely, but don't share private code or roadmaps until you've decided what's open source.
6. Journal every investor conversation (section 9) the same day: what was offered, what you felt, what you decided.

**Where you need a lawyer:** forming or converting the company, founder vesting and IP assignment, any SAFE or side letter, any studio or services-for-equity deal, any design partner agreement that touches IP or data, and before you publish anything as open source (the license choice is a legal choice). Many startup firms offer founder packages or deferred fees, and Austin has several. Asking Antler or other founders for referrals is normal.

## 8. First users: the design-partner relationship

**The standard name for what you described is a design partner:** an early user who gets the product early (often free or discounted) and in return commits to regular feedback, so you can do Discovery with them while building for them. It's not an investor, an employee or a co-founder, and it shouldn't involve equity.

| Relationship | What they get | What you get | Equity | Fits now? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| User | Access under plain terms | Usage, maybe feedback | No | Later, once Naive is released |
| Design partner | Early access, direct line to you, influence on the roadmap, a founder discount later | Regular interviews, real use, permission to learn from their venture, a case study or reference if it works | No | Yes, the usual choice for a first user |
| Pilot customer | A paid trial with defined scope | Early revenue and a pricing signal | No | Possibly, if you want a pricing test |
| Advisor | Small vesting equity | Ongoing advice | Under 1%, vesting | Only if they're advising you, not using Naive |
| Partner (co-builder) | Shared ownership of something | Their product or channel | Possibly | Not until vision and open source are decided |

**A design-partner package (`partners`, proposed).** Like the investor packages, it's a Naive definition with a Mentura kit:

- **Records:** one per partner: their venture, phase, what they need from Naive, cadence, consent for what you may record or share, and a log of each session.
- **Template:** a one-page design-partner agreement covering duration (often 3 to 6 months), feedback cadence, confidentiality both ways, who owns feedback and ideas (you, so you can build on them), how their data is handled, what they'll pay or the discount after, and how either side can end it. A lawyer should review it once; then you reuse it.
- **Checks:** no partner session starts without signed consent; their venture's data stays in their workspace, never in yours (fails closed).
- **Kit (Mentura):** interview prompts drawn from your Discovery questions, a session summary written into your `evidence/`, and a needs map across partners that shows patterns.

This is also the strongest thing you can bring an investor: "Five founders are using it weekly and here's what they told me." Design partners turn your Discovery into evidence.

## 9. The founder journal

Your own words, from the message that started this: a founder needs "somewhere that you keep your own journal at every phase of the venture. It helps you to be able to look back and reflect on why you made each decision."

**Proposal: a `journal` package in the founder suite.**

- **Records:** dated entries in the founder's words, never rewritten by an agent. Each entry carries the venture's phase at the time and can link to the decisions, conversations and evidence it's about.
- **Private by default:** the journal is marked `private` and is never included in a release, an investor update or anything an agent shares. The founder can choose to quote an entry.
- **Prompts, not answers:** the kit (Mentura) can offer a question after key moments (an investor meeting, a decision accepted, a phase change), such as "What did you feel, and what did you decide?" It never drafts the entry for you.
- **Reflection:** at a phase review, the kit can show entries next to the decisions they led to, so you can see why you chose what you did. This answers the "why" behind each decision, which the decision records alone don't hold.
- **Fit with what's built:** decisions already record your words in their answers (EE-054). The journal is the layer underneath: what you were thinking before anything became a decision.
